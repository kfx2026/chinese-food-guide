Frequently Asked Questions

Everything you want to know about authentic Chinese cuisine

1. What are the main regional cuisines of China?

China has eight major regional culinary traditions, each shaped by geography, climate, and history: Sichuan cuisine (famous for spicy, numbing mala flavors using Sichuan peppercorns and chilies), Cantonese cuisine (light, fresh, seafood-focused with iconic dim sum), Shandong cuisine (savory, seafood-rich, the foundation of northern Chinese cooking), Jiangsu cuisine (delicate, slightly sweet, known for braising techniques), Zhejiang cuisine (fresh, light, specializing in bamboo shoots and freshwater fish), Fujian cuisine (umami-rich, soup-based, famous for Buddha Jumps Over the Wall), Hunan cuisine (intensely hot, sour, and smoked flavors), and Anhui cuisine (wild herbs, mountain ingredients, slow-braising traditions). Beyond these eight, every province has unique local specialties worth exploring in our directory.

2. What is the most famous Chinese dish?

Peking Duck is arguably the most famous Chinese dish globally—crispy lacquered skin, tender meat, served with thin wheat pancakes, fresh scallions, cucumber, and sweet bean sauce. Kung Pao Chicken (spicy diced chicken with peanuts) and Mapo Tofu (silken tofu in fiery chili bean paste) are beloved Sichuan classics. Xiao Long Bao (Shanghai soup dumplings with pork and scalding broth inside) and Hot Pot (communal tableside cooking in bubbling broth) have exploded in global popularity. But Chinese cuisine encompasses thousands of regional dishes—our directory covers specialties from all 34 provinces, many of which are rarely seen outside China.

3. Is Chinese food healthy?

Traditional Chinese home cooking is fundamentally balanced and health-conscious, emphasizing variety across grains (rice, wheat, millet), seasonal vegetables, and moderate portions of meat or seafood. Steaming, stir-frying (quick cooking that preserves nutrients), and braising are the dominant techniques. Cantonese cuisine is particularly health-oriented with a focus on fresh ingredients and light preparation. However, restaurant-style Chinese food—especially overseas adaptations—often uses more oil, salt, and sugar than traditional versions. Authentic Chinese cooking philosophy follows the principle of food as medicine, with ingredients selected for their warming or cooling properties according to traditional Chinese medicine.

4. What are essential Chinese cooking techniques?

Stir-frying (chao) is the most iconic: ingredients move constantly in a blazing-hot wok with a small amount of oil for maximum flavor in minimum time. Steaming (zheng) preserves delicate flavors—essential for whole fish and dim sum. Red-braising (hong shao) involves slow-cooking meat in soy sauce, sugar, and spices until deeply colored and tender. Velveting coats proteins in egg white and cornstarch before cooking, creating the silky texture of restaurant stir-fries. Other techniques include dry-frying, smoking (tea-smoked duck), and double-boiling for soups. The wok is the essential tool—its shape enables multiple techniques including steaming (with a rack), smoking, and deep-frying. Wok hei (breath of the wok)—the smoky, caramelized flavor from extreme heat—is the hallmark of masterful stir-frying.

5. What is the difference between Chinese food in China versus overseas?

The gap is significant. Overseas Chinese food has been profoundly adapted to local palates over generations. Dishes like General Tso's Chicken, Chop Suey, Crab Rangoon, and fortune cookies were all invented outside China—they simply don't exist in Chinese restaurants in China. Authentic regional Chinese cuisine uses ingredients rarely seen on Western menus: century eggs (preserved duck eggs), bitter melon, fermented black beans, wood ear fungus, lotus root, chicken feet, and dozens of types of fresh and dried tofu. The spice level is dramatically different—Sichuan mala is genuinely intense, not watered down for export. Regional differences in China are as stark as the differences between French and Italian cuisine—our directory helps you understand what real Chinese food tastes like.

6. Are there vegetarian options in Chinese cuisine?

Absolutely. Chinese Buddhist vegetarian cuisine is a sophisticated 1,500-year tradition that uses tofu, wheat gluten (seitan), mushrooms, bamboo shoots, and vegetables to create dishes that mimic meat textures and flavors. Landmark vegetarian dishes include Buddha's Delight (a mixed vegetable stew traditionally served on Chinese New Year), Mapo Tofu made without ground pork, Dry-Fried Green Beans with pickled vegetables, Fish-Fragrant Eggplant (yu xiang qie zi—no fish, just the seasoning), and countless tofu preparations from silken to extra-firm. Many Buddhist temples in China operate excellent vegetarian restaurants, and mainstream restaurants can usually adapt dishes upon request.

7. What are common Chinese spices and seasonings?

A well-stocked Chinese pantry centers on soy sauce (light for seasoning, dark for color), Shaoxing rice wine (for deglazing and marinades), Chinkiang black vinegar (complex, slightly sweet), Sichuan peppercorns (producing a unique numbing sensation called mala), star anise and cassia cinnamon (for braises), five-spice powder (star anise, cloves, cinnamon, Sichuan pepper, fennel), fermented broad bean paste (doubanjiang—the soul of Sichuan cooking), oyster sauce, hoisin sauce, sesame oil, and fresh aromatics (ginger, garlic, scallions). These ingredients form the foundation of Chinese flavor profiles. Most are now widely available in Asian grocery stores internationally.

8. What is the proper way to eat a Chinese meal?

Chinese meals are fundamentally communal and shared. Dishes are placed in the center of the table, and everyone takes small portions using serving chopsticks or the reverse end of their own chopsticks onto their personal rice bowl. Soup is traditionally served last, signaling the meal's end. It's considered polite to try every dish served and to leave a small amount—showing the host has provided amply. Never stick chopsticks vertically into rice (this resembles funeral incense) and avoid pointing with chopsticks. Tipping is not customary in China. The meal is as much about conversation and relationship-building as about food itself.

9. What is the most expensive Chinese dish?

Buddha Jumps Over the Wall (Fo Tiao Qiang), originating from Fujian province, can cost thousands of dollars per pot. This legendary soup combines over 30 premium ingredients including abalone, shark fin (increasingly replaced with sustainable alternatives), sea cucumber, dried scallops, fish maw, Jinhua ham, and ginseng, requiring 2-3 days of preparation. Bird's Nest Soup (made from swiftlet nests) is another luxury item prized for its delicate texture and alleged health benefits. High-grade dried seafood—particularly abalone and sea cucumber—can cost hundreds of dollars per pound. These are banquet dishes reserved for the most important occasions and wealthy diners.

10. How do I find authentic Chinese restaurants in my city?

Look for restaurants where Chinese families dine, not just Western tourists. Signs of authenticity include: menus featuring regional specialties (Sichuan, Hunan, Cantonese) rather than just generic 'Chinese food,' a separate Chinese-language menu or specials board, dishes like cold appetizers, whole fish, and organ meats, an absence of fortune cookies, and servers who can discuss regional differences. Online Chinese community apps and forums are excellent resources—local Chinese residents know exactly where to find real food from home. Our directory also includes recipes so you can cook authentic regional dishes yourself, bypassing restaurant compromises entirely.
